  {\large \textbf{Organic Chemistry: Reactions and Mechanisms -- Answer Key}}\\[0.5em]

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]

  \item \textbf{(B)} The structure of the alkyl halide (primary, secondary, tertiary).\\
  Primary alkyl halides favor $\mathrm{S_N2}$ (backside attack possible), while tertiary halides 
  favor $\mathrm{S_N1}$ (stable carbocation intermediate). Secondary halides can proceed by either 
  mechanism depending on conditions. Steric hindrance and carbocation stability are the key factors.

  \item \textbf{(C)} Halogen atoms (e.g., $-\mathrm{Cl}$, $-\mathrm{Br}$).\\
  Halogens are ortho/para-directing due to resonance donation of lone pair electrons, but they are 
  deactivating due to their electron-withdrawing inductive effect. This makes them unique among 
  substituents.

  \item \textbf{(A)} 1-bromobutane.\\
  Under anti-Markovnikov conditions (peroxide effect), the radical mechanism causes Br to add to 
  the less substituted carbon (terminal position), yielding 1-bromobutane instead of the Markovnikov 
  product 2-bromobutane.

  \item \textbf{(C)} Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy.\\
  NMR (especially $^1$H and $^{13}$C NMR) reveals connectivity by showing which atoms are bonded 
  to which, through chemical shifts, coupling patterns, and integration. This makes it ideal for 
  distinguishing constitutional isomers.

  \item \emph{Sample answer:}\\
  \textbf{Carbocation stability}: Carbocations are stabilized by:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hyperconjugation}: Adjacent C-H or C-C bonds donate electron density
    \item \textbf{Inductive effects}: Alkyl groups are electron-donating
    \item Stability order: tertiary $>$ secondary $>$ primary $>$ methyl
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Markovnikov's rule}: In the addition of HX to an alkene, the hydrogen adds to the carbon 
  with more hydrogens (less substituted), and X adds to the more substituted carbon. This occurs 
  because:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The mechanism proceeds through the more stable carbocation intermediate
    \item Addition to the more substituted position forms a more stable carbocation
    \item The reaction follows the pathway with the lowest energy intermediate
  \end{itemize}

  \item \emph{Sample answer:}\\
  \textbf{Aldol condensation mechanism}:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Base abstracts an $\alpha$-hydrogen from a carbonyl compound, forming an enolate ion
    \item The enolate acts as a nucleophile, attacking the carbonyl carbon of another molecule
    \item Protonation yields the aldol adduct (a $\beta$-hydroxy carbonyl compound)
    \item Under heating or acidic/basic conditions, dehydration occurs (elimination of water)
    \item This forms the $\alpha,\beta$-unsaturated carbonyl product (conjugated system)
  \end{enumerate}

  \textbf{Conditions favoring unsaturated product}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Elevated temperature promotes dehydration
    \item The $\alpha,\beta$-unsaturated product is stabilized by conjugation
    \item Removal of water (Le Chatelier's principle) drives the equilibrium
  \end{itemize}

  \item \emph{Sample answer:}\\
  \textbf{Kinetic control}: At low temperature or short reaction time, the product formed fastest 
  predominates (lowest activation energy path). This may not be the most stable product.

  \textbf{Thermodynamic control}: At high temperature or with longer time (allowing equilibration), 
  the most stable product predominates (lowest free energy).

  \textbf{Example - Sulfonation of naphthalene}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item At $80^\circ$C: 1-naphthalenesulfonic acid forms faster (kinetic product, less hindered position)
    \item At $160^\circ$C: 2-naphthalenesulfonic acid predominates (thermodynamic product, more stable 
    due to less steric strain)
    \item The reaction is reversible, allowing equilibration to the more stable isomer at higher temperature
  \end{itemize}

  \item \emph{Sample answer:}\\
  Acidity increases when the conjugate base is more stable due to resonance or inductive effects:

  \textbf{Ethanol} ($\mathrm{pK_a} \approx 16$): The ethoxide ion (EtO$^-$) has limited stabilization. 
  The negative charge is localized on oxygen with only inductive effects from the alkyl group.

  \textbf{Phenol} ($\mathrm{pK_a} \approx 10$): The phenoxide ion is stabilized by resonance with 
  the aromatic ring. The negative charge delocalizes over multiple carbon atoms via resonance structures, 
  significantly lowering the energy of the conjugate base.

  \textbf{Acetic acid} ($\mathrm{pK_a} \approx 4.75$): The acetate ion is stabilized by resonance 
  between two equivalent oxygen atoms. The negative charge is equally shared between both oxygens, 
  providing strong stabilization.

  \textbf{Acidity order}: Acetic acid $>$ Phenol $>$ Ethanol. Greater resonance stabilization of 
  the conjugate base leads to higher acidity.

\end{enumerate}
