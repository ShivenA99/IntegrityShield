  {\Large \textbf{PHYS 455 Examination}}\\[0.25em]
  {\large \textbf{Quantum Mechanics and Wave Functions -- Answer Key}}\\[0.5em]
\end{center}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]

  \item \textbf{(B)} $\hbar/2$.\\
  The Heisenberg uncertainty principle states $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \geq \hbar/2$, where 
  $\hbar = h/2\pi$ is the reduced Planck constant. This minimum is achieved for Gaussian wave packets.

  \item \textbf{(B)} They scale as $n^2$ where $n$ is the quantum number.\\
  For the infinite square well, $E_n = \frac{n^2 \pi^2 \hbar^2}{2mL^2}$ where $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ 
  The energies are quantized and scale quadratically with $n$, not evenly spaced.

  \item \textbf{(C)} $|\psi(x,t)|^2$ represents the probability density for finding the particle at 
  position $x$.\\
  The Born rule states that $|\psi(x,t)|^2 dx$ gives the probability of finding the particle in the 
  interval $[x, x+dx]$ at time $t$. The wave function itself is generally complex-valued.

  \item \textbf{(B)} It has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenfunctions.\\
  Hermitian operators satisfy $\langle \phi | \hat{A} \psi \rangle = \langle \hat{A} \phi | \psi \rangle$, 
  which guarantees real eigenvalues (measurable quantities) and orthogonal eigenstates (distinct measurement 
  outcomes are distinguishable).

  \item \emph{Sample answer:}\\
  The commutator $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = \hat{A}\hat{B} - \hat{B}\hat{A}$ measures whether two observables 
  can be simultaneously measured with arbitrary precision:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = 0$, the observables are compatible: they share common eigenstates 
    and can be simultaneously measured with definite values
    \item If $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] \neq 0$, the observables are incompatible: measuring one disturbs 
    the other, and they cannot both have definite values simultaneously
    \item Example: $[\hat{x}, \hat{p}] = i\hbar$ leads to the uncertainty principle 
    $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \geq \hbar/2$
  \end{itemize}
  This is fundamental to quantum complementarity and distinguishes quantum from classical physics.

  \item \emph{Sample answer:}\\
  The quantum harmonic oscillator describes a particle in a quadratic potential $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x^2$:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Energy spectrum}: $E_n = \hbar\omega(n + 1/2)$ for $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ The energies 
    are evenly spaced, and the ground state has non-zero energy $E_0 = \hbar\omega/2$ (zero-point energy)
    \item \textbf{Wave functions}: Involve Hermite polynomials and Gaussians, symmetric (even $n$) or 
    antisymmetric (odd $n$)
    \item \textbf{Significance}: Models molecular vibrations, phonons in solids, quantum field theory modes, 
    and electromagnetic field oscillations. It's exactly solvable and exhibits key quantum features like 
    quantization and zero-point motion
  \end{itemize}

  \item \emph{Sample answer:}\\
  \textbf{Time-dependent Schr\"odinger equation}:
  $$i\hbar \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = \hat{H}\psi$$
  This governs the time evolution of quantum states and is the equation of motion analogous to Newton's 
  second law in classical mechanics.

  \textbf{Time-independent version}: When the Hamiltonian is time-independent, we can separate variables: 
  $\psi(x,t) = \phi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, leading to:
  $$\hat{H}\phi = E\phi$$
  This eigenvalue equation determines stationary states (energy eigenstates).

  \textbf{When to use each}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Time-independent: Finding energy eigenstates, bound state problems, stationary potentials
    \item Time-dependent: Evolution of superpositions, time-varying potentials, scattering dynamics
  \end{itemize}

  \item \emph{Sample answer:}\\
  \textbf{Quantum tunneling}: A quantum particle can penetrate and traverse potential barriers even when 
  its energy $E$ is less than the barrier height $V_0$ (classically forbidden).

  \textbf{Wave nature explanation}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The wave function doesn't abruptly vanish at a barrier; it decays exponentially inside the 
    barrier region as $\psi \sim e^{-\kappa x}$ where $\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$
    \item If the barrier is finite in width, the wave function has non-zero amplitude on the other side
    \item This leads to non-zero transmission probability even for $E < V_0$
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Implications}: Tunneling enables radioactive alpha decay, scanning tunneling microscopy (STM), 
  tunnel diodes, and is essential in nuclear fusion in stars. It has no classical analog and demonstrates 
  the wave-particle duality of quantum mechanics.

\end{enumerate}
