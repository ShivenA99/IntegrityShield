  {\large \textbf{Modern European and World History (1789--1945) -- Answer Key}}\\[0.5em]

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]

  \item \textbf{(B)} Wilson's Fourteen Points.\\
  President Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points (January 1918) emphasized national self-determination, 
  calling for the adjustment of colonial claims with consideration for the peoples concerned. This 
  principle shaped the redrawing of European boundaries after WWI, though imperfectly implemented.

  \item \textbf{(A)} Overproduction and underconsumption combined with speculation and credit expansion.\\
  The 1920s saw overproduction in agriculture and industry, speculation in stocks (buying on margin), 
  and unequal wealth distribution limiting consumption. When the stock market crashed in October 1929, 
  credit contracted, demand collapsed, and the downward spiral began.

  \item \textbf{(B)} The French Revolution (1789).\\
  The French Revolution's principles of liberty, equality, and fraternity, along with popular 
  sovereignty replacing divine right, inspired revolutionary and nationalist movements across Europe 
  throughout the 19th century.

  \item \textbf{(B)} Neville Chamberlain.\\
  Chamberlain pursued appeasement, most notably agreeing to German annexation of the Sudetenland at 
  the Munich Conference (September 1938), believing it would preserve peace. The policy failed when 
  Hitler invaded Czechoslovakia and Poland.

  \item \emph{Sample answer:}\\
  \textbf{Causes}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Agricultural improvements freed labor for industry
    \item Capital accumulation from trade and colonialism
    \item Technological innovations: steam engine, mechanized textile production, iron and steel advances
    \item Coal and iron resources, especially in Britain
    \item Political stability and favorable legal frameworks (property rights, patents)
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Economic transformation}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Shift from agrarian to industrial economy
    \item Factory system replaced cottage industry
    \item Mass production lowered costs, increased output
    \item Expansion of trade and transportation (railroads, steamships)
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Social consequences}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Urbanization: mass migration to cities (Manchester, Birmingham)
    \item Emergence of industrial working class with poor conditions (long hours, child labor, unsafe factories)
    \item Growth of middle class (factory owners, merchants, professionals)
    \item Social problems: overcrowding, disease, pollution
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Political effects}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Rise of labor movements and trade unions demanding reforms
    \item Emergence of socialist ideologies (Marx, Engels)
    \item Reform legislation: Factory Acts limiting work hours, improving conditions
    \item Extension of suffrage as industrial workers gained political voice
  \end{itemize}

  \item \emph{Sample answer:}\\
  \textbf{Origins}: Established at the Congress of Vienna (1814--1815) after Napoleon's defeat. The 
  major powers (Austria, Britain, Prussia, Russia, later France) agreed to meet periodically to 
  maintain the balance of power and suppress revolutionary movements.

  \textbf{Principles}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Legitimacy}: Restore legitimate monarchs overthrown by Napoleon
    \item \textbf{Balance of power}: Ensure no single power dominates Europe
    \item \textbf{Collective security}: Major powers intervene to prevent revolution and maintain order
    \item \textbf{Conservatism}: Preserve traditional institutions against liberal and nationalist movements
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Significance}: Maintained relative peace in Europe (1815--1854), preventing general war. 
  Congresses at Aix-la-Chapelle, Troppau, and Verona addressed crises.

  \textbf{Limitations}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Could not permanently suppress nationalism and liberalism
    \item Britain withdrew from interventionist policies (Crimean War broke the system)
    \item Ignored aspirations of people for self-determination
    \item Eventually undermined by nationalist movements (Italian and German unification, Greek independence)
  \end{itemize}

  \item \emph{Sample answer:}\\
  \textbf{Alliance system}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Triple Alliance: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy (1882)
    \item Triple Entente: France, Russia, Britain (1907)
    \item Created rigid, interlocking commitments that turned a regional crisis into a general war
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Militarism}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Arms race, especially naval competition between Britain and Germany
    \item Large standing armies with mobilization plans (Schlieffen Plan)
    \item Military leaders influenced policy, favored offensive strategies
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Imperialism}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Competition for colonies in Africa and Asia created tensions
    \item Moroccan crises (1905, 1911) between France and Germany
    \item Desire for resources, markets, and prestige
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Nationalism}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Balkan nationalism destabilized Austria-Hungary (Slavic peoples seeking independence)
    \item Pan-Slavism (Russian support for Slavs) vs. Pan-Germanism
    \item Assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand by Serbian nationalist (June 1914) triggered the war
  \end{itemize}

  These factors created a powder keg; the assassination provided the spark.

  \item \emph{Sample answer:}\\
  \textbf{Common factors across regimes}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Economic crisis and instability (postwar chaos, Great Depression)
    \item Weak democratic institutions or traditions
    \item Fear of communism or social revolution
    \item National humiliation or unfulfilled nationalist aspirations
    \item Use of mass propaganda and modern communication
    \item Charismatic leadership exploiting crises
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Italy (Mussolini, 1922)}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Postwar economic problems, unemployment, strikes
    \item Fear of socialist revolution
    \item Disappointment over ``mutilated victory'' (territorial gains at Versailles)
    \item Weak liberal government unable to maintain order
    \item Mussolini's Fascist squadristi used violence against socialists
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Germany (Hitler, 1933)}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Treaty of Versailles seen as humiliating (war guilt, reparations, territorial losses)
    \item Hyperinflation (1923) destroyed middle-class savings
    \item Great Depression: massive unemployment (6 million by 1932)
    \item Weak Weimar democracy with proportional representation
    \item Hitler promised restoration of German greatness, scapegoated Jews
  \end{itemize}

  \textbf{Soviet Union (Stalin, 1920s--1930s)}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Bolshevik seizure of power created one-party state (1917)
    \item Civil War (1918--1921) militarized politics
    \item Lenin's death (1924) led to power struggle; Stalin eliminated rivals
    \item Collectivization and industrialization enforced through terror
    \item No democratic tradition, tsarist legacy of autocracy
  \end{itemize}

  All three leaders consolidated power through propaganda, secret police, suppression of opposition, 
  and cult of personality.

\end{enumerate}
